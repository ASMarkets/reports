01The question it asks

Every position, eventually, faces the same question — win or lose: would you buy this again, today, at today's price, knowing everything you know now? That's the Today Test. It doesn't ask what a position is worth. It asks one thing only: is this still a business worth owning, today, judged with no memory of what was paid for it?

02The position, framed without a price tag

In this case, a position was opened seven trading days before the report — small, on purpose, the size this process only ever risks going into a binary event like an earnings report. No cushion built in. Position size is never stated here in dollars or share count, by design — only that it was intentionally small, and that the entry sat inside a window this same case would go on to close for good, permanently, going forward (see section 06).

03What the test found

The test ran in the days after the drop. The balance sheet hadn't changed — still no debt, still cash on hand. But the reason for owning it had. Management confirmed, on the record, that growth was paused, not accelerating, and the thesis that justified the entry no longer matched what the company was actually saying about itself.

“Is this still a business worth owning, today — blind to what it already cost you?”
Today Test: No

Here, the answer came back no — not because the loss was large, but because the reason for holding had already stopped being true.

04A later signal — and why it doesn't undo the verdict

One more fact worth naming, because it tests the discipline itself, not just the stock. In the days right after the drop, company directors who'd been silent for three months started buying — five separate purchases from three different insiders within two and a half weeks.

That doesn't undo a Today Test that already ran on the facts available at the time. It's new information, arriving after the verdict — and the discipline is to re-run the test, not to quietly assume the old answer still holds forever. A process that updates when the facts do isn't indecisive. A process that never updates is just stubborn.

05What the three checkpoints, together, prove

Put the three checkpoints together, and here's what an earnings event actually tests — not whether a stock goes up or down on the day, but whether a process stays honest before, during, and after the surprise.

06The rule this case created

This is, in fact, the exact kind of case that led this process to add a rule it didn't have before:

The rule this case created

No new position within a set number of trading sessions of a confirmed earnings date — unless the pre-earnings checkpoint gives a clear green light.

The report didn't create this risk. It only made it impossible to keep ignoring.
